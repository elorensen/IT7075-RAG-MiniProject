# Fictional SOC Guide: Security Awareness and Reporting

## Program goals
This original educational guide describes a fictional company's awareness program. Its primary goal is to help employees recognize suspicious activity, report it quickly, and understand what happens after a report. Training should emphasize practical actions rather than fear or punishment. This document deliberately does not provide an annual training budget or a numeric spending target.

## Employee phishing reporting
Employees should report suspicious messages using the approved phishing-reporting button or security help channel, not forward the message to an uncontrolled distribution list. If a suspicious link was clicked, the employee should report that fact immediately; early reporting supports containment. Employees should avoid deleting the original message until the security team advises them to do so. Reports should include what was clicked or entered if known, without sending the actual password.

## Recognizing suspicious requests
Common warning signs include unexpected attachments, urgent payment requests, altered sender addresses, unusual login requests, and demands to bypass standard procedures. These indicators are not perfect: legitimate messages can be urgent, and attackers can compromise real accounts. Employees should verify unusual requests through a previously trusted communication channel rather than replying to the suspicious email.

## Credential safety
Never share a password, recovery code, or multifactor authentication approval with another person over email or chat. An unexpected multifactor prompt should be denied and reported immediately. When a user suspects credential exposure, the security team coordinates approved password-reset and session-revocation procedures. Training examples use made-up names and accounts rather than real employee information.

## Training delivery and measurement
New employees receive a short introduction to phishing reporting and multifactor authentication during onboarding. Periodic refreshers use sanitized examples to reinforce the same reporting path. Program effectiveness can be evaluated using reporting timeliness, participation, and qualitative feedback. A high click count should trigger supportive follow-up education, not a public list shaming employees.

## Escalation and recordkeeping
Awareness reports that indicate an actual account compromise are handed to the incident-response queue. The awareness coordinator records the date and general topic of each training activity. The program owner reviews recurring confusion and updates guidance when procedures change. No specific monthly schedule, vendor contract, or financial allocation is defined in this fictional document.
